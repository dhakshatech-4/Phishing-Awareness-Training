Security awareness training

Every attack starts with a message someone almost believed.

Phishing doesn't rely on breaking your systems — it relies on you clicking, replying, or trusting for just one second longer than you should. This module walks through what to look for, why the tricks work, and what to do instead. About 15 minutes, one quiz at the end.

Exhibit B — address bar

Fake websites live in the URL, not the design

A cloned login page can be pixel-perfect. The giveaway is almost always the domain itself — read it slowly, right to left from the first single slash.

🔓 https://accounts-secure-login.paypa1-verify.com
PayPal

Confirm your identity to restore account access

Impersonation domain
🔒 https://www.paypal.com/signin
PayPal

Log in to your account

Legitimate domain
The real domain is exactly paypal.com. The fake one strings extra words before a look-alike domain (paypa1-verify.com, with a digit "1" standing in for "l") — the part that matters is always the piece right before the first single "/". A padlock icon only means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is trustworthy; attackers buy valid certificates too.
Under the hood

The psychology attackers lean on

Phishing is social engineering delivered by email, text, or phone. These are the emotional levers that show up again and again, often stacked together.

Urgency clock

A deadline, expiring account, or "act now" framing that pressures you to skip your normal checks.

"Your access will be suspended in 2 hours."

Authority badge

The message appears to come from a boss, executive, IT, or a well-known brand — people comply faster with perceived power.

"This is the CEO — I need a favor before my flight."

Fear alert

A threat of consequences — a fine, a locked account, legal trouble, a security incident — that triggers a reflexive response.

"Unusual sign-in detected on your account."

Curiosity eye

Something too intriguing to ignore, like a shared document, a delivery, or a photo you're tagged in.

"See who viewed your profile this week."

Familiarity link

Spoofed identities of coworkers, vendors, or services you already use, so the request feels routine.

"Following up on the invoice from last week."

Reciprocity gift

An unsolicited favor, refund, or gift that creates a subtle obligation to respond quickly.

"You've been selected for a reward — claim by Friday."
Exhibit C — other doors

Email is only the front entrance

The same six levers from the previous section get reused across every channel your phone and desk can reach. What changes is the surface — and the fact that people are more relaxed on a small screen, mid-task, with no time to hover a link.

📱

Smishing

SMS / WhatsApp

Text messages and WhatsApp chats carrying the same lures. The medium is trusted and the screen is small, which is exactly why it works so well.

INR 4,99,999 has been credited to your HDFC account. To complete KYC, update your details within 24 hours: hdfc-kyc-verify.in/claims
Telltales — unsolicited prize or refund news; a short link with no clear destination; a count-down deadline; requests to "share OTP" or enter a PIN, which no bank will ever ask for.
📞

Vishing

Voice call

A phone call that borrows authority and familiarity — a fake bank fraud desk, a spoofed internal helpdesk, a "senior leader" asking for a favour.

"This is the fraud department. Your card is compromised. To secure it I need you to read the six-digit code that just arrived on your phone back to me."
Telltales — caller ID is spoofable, so a familiar number proves nothing; you are never asked to read out an OTP; pressure to "act before the money leaves" creates manufactured urgency.
📷

Quishing

QR code

A QR code in a PDF, a poster, a sticker in a car park, or an email. Scanning jumps you straight to a login page with no address bar to inspect first.

Physical sticker on a parking meter: "Scan to pay 50% less — scan to claim your free voucher"
Telltales — codes to "avoid a service fee" or claim a reward; anywhere that genuinely needs payment already has a phone number and a printed receipt; the destination can't be checked until after you've landed on it.
💬

Phishing via chat & social

Instant messaging

Attackers compromise a real friend's account and send a message from it. Because the contact is genuine, your normal scepticism switches off completely.

A known contact, from a number you've messaged for years: "I'm in an emergency, can't talk — can you send me ₹20,000 right now? Don't tell anyone."
Telltales — secrecy plus urgency is a classic pairing; a new account in a group chat asking people to "verify details"; a "shared document" that needs you to "sign in again".
📞

Deepfake voice & video

Emerging

Short voice-cloning clips built from public audio can now imitate a familiar voice convincingly enough for a payment approval. Treat any voice-based request as one channel of evidence, not two.

A familiar voice, "Hi, it's me. I'm in a meeting, just approve the payment on my behalf, I'll explain later."
Telltales — the call arrives with a second, unrelated request; the caller is oddly unbothered by being questioned; the pressure is to complete it quickly, right now.
📰

Search & sponsored ads

Malvertising

Fake "support" phone numbers and update pages bought at the top of search results for a brand you just called. You searched, so the URL looks plausible.

Searching for "yourbrand" customer care number → top result is a sponsored ad leading to yourbrand-helpdesk-support.net
Telltales — the number differs from the one on the back of your card; the page asks for a password or OTP "for verification"; sponsored results are ads, not editorial ranking.
The one rule that covers every channel

If a message creates a feeling you should act right now — money moving, an account closing, a leader waiting, a prize expiring — that feeling is the payload. Any request that carries urgency gets verified through a channel that did not initiate it, before a single detail is entered.

From the field

Three ways this plays out

Composite scenarios based on patterns seen across many real incidents.

The "urgent wire transfer"

Business email compromise

A finance employee receives an email that appears to be from their CEO, sent while she is "in back-to-back meetings," asking for a same-day payment to a new vendor account. The tone is terse and time-pressured, matching how the real executive writes — attackers had studied her public talks and old emails first.

Lesson — Any request to move money or change payment details gets verified through a second channel, like a phone call to a known number, no matter who appears to be asking.

The cloned login page

Credential harvesting

Employees get an email about a shared file that needs "re-authentication." The link leads to a login page identical to the company's real single sign-on screen, hosted on a domain that differs by one hyphen. Credentials entered there are captured instantly and often replayed within minutes.

Lesson — Reach internal tools by typing the known address or using a saved bookmark, never by clicking a link in an email asking you to "re-verify."

The MFA fatigue call

Vishing

After stolen credentials trigger repeated login prompts, the target's phone rings. The caller, posing as IT support, apologizes for "the flood of notifications" and asks them to approve one so the system can be fixed. It's the same push notification the attacker is trying to get approved.

Lesson — IT will never ask you to approve a login you didn't initiate. Unexpected MFA prompts get denied and reported, not approved to make them stop.

The invoice that matched perfectly

Vendor impersonation

A recurring monthly invoice arrives from the correct vendor name, the correct logo, and a signature block copied from real previous emails — only the bank account details are wrong. The invoice amount, PO number, and line items are all legitimate, so nothing looks unusual. Payments go out on the usual date, which means nobody is scrutinising this one.

Lesson — Any change to bank account details for a payment gets confirmed by phone against a number you already had on file. A one-time phone call prevents an entire year of wrong payouts.

If it already happened

You clicked. Now what?

The most useful thing you can do is stop and report quickly. Every minute matters, and you will not be punished for reporting — organisations can only protect you if they know. Work down this list in order.

  1. immediately Disconnect from the network if you downloaded something

    If you ran an attachment or installed software, unplug the ethernet cable and switch off Wi-Fi. Don't keep browsing — attackers who gain a foothold start moving laterally within minutes.

  2. within 5 minutes Report it — before you delete anything

    Use the phishing-report button in your mail client, or forward it to your security team. Reporting blocks the sender for everyone else who received the same message. Deleting first destroys the evidence and the headers investigators need.

  3. within 5 minutes If you entered a password, change it now — from a different device

    Use a known-good device: another laptop or phone that wasn't involved. Change the password, then sign out of all active sessions wherever the service offers it, so a stolen session token dies with it.

  4. within 5 minutes If you entered an OTP or approved an MFA prompt, revoke the sessions and call the bank

    An OTP you handed over completes a login for the attacker. Call the number on the back of your card, not a number in the message, and ask for the account to be re-secured and any new device access removed.

  5. within 15 minutes If you entered card or bank details, freeze and dispute

    Call your bank's fraud line immediately. Most banks can stop a pending transaction, and reporting within hours materially improves your chance of a full reversal.

  6. same day Check for the slow, quiet compromise

    Attackers rarely stop at the first click. Review recent account activity: sent items, new inbox rules that auto-forward mail, changed recovery addresses, new payees, MFA prompts you don't recognise, and login history from unfamiliar locations.

  7. same day Tell your team if it was a shared or public link

    If the payload affected a document, drive, or internal tool, others may have been exposed through the same link. Early notice turns a contained incident into a small one.

  8. this week Write down what happened while it's fresh

    Time of the click, the exact link, what you typed, what you saw afterwards. A five-minute timeline helps investigators enormously and speeds up insurance or bank claims.

What will not happen

You will not be fired for reporting a click quickly. The expensive outcome is the one that stays unreported — an attacker moving quietly through mailboxes and payment systems for weeks. Fast, honest reporting is the single most valuable thing a person in an organisation can do after a mistake.

Field guide

Habits that hold up under pressure

  • ✓
    Check the actual sender address, not the display nameDisplay names are free to fake; the domain after the @ is what to read carefully.
  • ✓
    Hover before you clickOn desktop, hovering shows the real destination URL in the status bar before you commit.
  • ✓
    Go direct instead of following a linkFor anything account-related, open the site yourself or use a bookmark you already trust.
  • ✓
    Verify unusual requests out of bandA call or message through a separate, known channel confirms whether a request is real in seconds.
  • ✓
    Slow down when you feel rushedUrgency is a manufactured feeling, not a reason to skip a check — legitimate requests survive a two-minute pause.
  • ✓
    Report instead of just deletingReporting a phish gets it blocked for every colleague who received the same message.
Two habits that carry the most weight

Go direct. For anything account-related, open the app or type the address yourself. Most successful phishing chains are defeated entirely by a habit of navigating rather than clicking.

Verify out of band. A request that arrives on one channel gets confirmed on another — a phone call to a number you already had. This one habit defeats spear phishing, MFA fatigue, and business email compromise, none of which have a technical tell.

Pocket reference

The whole module on one screen

Everything from the sections above, condensed into six cards. Print this page or keep it beside your monitor — it takes about twenty seconds to run through before clicking anything unfamiliar.

✉️ In the message

  • Sender domain — read past the display name, all the way to the text after the @
  • Generic greeting — "Dear User" or "Valued Customer" instead of your name
  • Manufactured deadline — 24 hours, 3 hours, "final notice"
  • Threatening consequence — suspension, closure, legal action, salary hold
  • Unsolicited attachment — especially .html, .zip, .xlsm, or a password-protected PDF
  • Unexplained credential request — any prompt to "confirm" your password, PIN, or OTP

🔗 In the link

  • Hover first — the real destination appears before you commit to anything
  • Read the domain right to left — the true owner is the text just before the first single /
  • Look-alike characters — rn for m, 0 for O, 1 for l, vv for w
  • Extra words before the real name — paypal.com.secure-login.net is not paypal.com
  • URL shorteners — bit.ly and similar hide the destination entirely
  • No padlock reassurance — encryption only means the connection is private, not that the site is honest

📱 On your phone

  • Never read out an OTP — not to a bank, not to "IT", not to anyone
  • Long-press to preview the destination before opening a link in a message
  • Caller ID proves nothing — numbers are trivially spoofed
  • Reaching a real bank? Hang up and call the number on the back of your card
  • QR code posters offering fee-free payment or vouchers are a known scam format
  • Verify a new contact through a channel you already trust before sending anything

🧠 In your own reaction

  • Urgency is a signal — manufactured feelings are themselves a red flag
  • Authority is not proof — a title in a signature means nothing
  • Curiosity is the hook — "see who viewed your profile" is designed to bypass scepticism
  • Two-minute rule — a legitimate request survives a pause; a scam resists one
  • Ask one question — "can you confirm this another way?" defuses most attempts
  • Nothing is too trivial to verify — low-stakes phishing is often just a test

🛡️ If you're careful, still

  • Report, don't delete — protects everyone who got the same message
  • Change from a clean device if credentials or a code were entered
  • End active sessions, not just the password
  • Call the bank using the number on your card, not the message
  • Check for auto-forward rules and new recovery addresses
  • Write the timeline down while it's fresh

✅ Five-second scan

  • Who — is the domain after the @ genuinely theirs?
  • Where — where does the link actually go, before the first single /?
  • What — are they asking for a password, OTP, PIN, or payment?
  • When — is there a deadline designed to stop me thinking?
  • How sure — would I be suspicious of this exact request arriving by phone or in person?

Reference

Terms you'll hear in the wild

Security teams use these terms loosely, so it helps to know what they actually mean in practice.

phishing
A message that impersonates a trusted party to steal credentials, money, or information. The generic term for the whole family.
spear phishing
Phishing researched and written for one specific recipient, using real details about them. Far more successful than bulk mail.
whaling
Spear phishing aimed at senior executives — the people with authority to move money quickly.
vishing / smishing / quishing
Phishing by voice call, by SMS or instant message, and by QR code respectively.
smishing (SMS specifics)
Fake delivery, KYC, prize, and bank-alert texts. Aims you at a cloned page that captures OTP or card details.
malvertising
Malicious code delivered through online ads, including fake search results and sponsored "support" links.
bec
Business email compromise — a phishing variant aimed purely at inducing a payment or a change of bank details. No malware, no technical tell at all.
push bombing / MFA fatigue
Flooding a victim's phone with login prompts so they approve one to make the noise stop, handing the attacker a session.
look-alike domain
A domain built to resemble a real one — extra words, hyphens, or substituted characters — so it reads correctly at a glance.
typosquatting
Registering a domain that is a plausible misspelling of a popular site, to catch people typing quickly.
credential harvesting
Any page whose only purpose is to collect a username and password, usually cloned from a real login screen.
session token
A value that proves you're logged in. Stealing it grants access without the password, which is why you must end sessions, not just change passwords.
pretexting
The invented story the attacker tells to justify their request. The pretext is what makes the urgency feel reasonable.
The whole module in one line

Attackers have better tools than you do. What they don't have is a legitimate reason for the request they're making — so give that reason a second source before you act on it, and the advantage becomes yours.
